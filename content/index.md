Most doctors use AI like a search box. You open it, type a question, read the answer, and close it. That is fine, and it is a good start. But it is the smallest thing these tools can do, and it means you redo the same setup work every single time.

There is a bigger way to use it. Instead of explaining yourself over and over, you set the AI up once with the background it needs and a safe way to reach your real work. After that it behaves less like a stranger you brief from scratch and more like a sharp assistant who already knows your practice, can see your files, and gets things done.

That setup has a name: **context engineering**. It sounds technical. It is not. It just means giving the AI the right background, once, in a place it can find it.

## Who this is for

This wiki is written for practicing physicians who are not programmers. You will not need to write code. Every page uses plain language, and every example is something you can copy, paste, and use the same day. If a term sounds technical, it is in the [[Glossary]].

It was built by Dr. Alfred-Marc (Jaymarc) Iloreta, an ENT and skull base surgeon at Mount Sinai, after colleagues asked how he organizes research, projects, and administrative work with AI. The examples lean surgical, but the method works for any specialty.

## Why a physician should care

AI is most valuable in the hands of someone with deep expertise in a field the tools have barely touched. Medicine, and surgery especially, is that kind of field.

The models are very capable in general, but general is not the same as useful to you. What makes them useful is your knowledge: how you work up a case, how you write a note, what a good plan looks like, what is safe and what is not. That knowledge is the scarce ingredient, and you already have it. A physician who learns to hand that knowledge to AI gets leverage that a generic tool can never give a generic user.

Think about where your time actually goes: documentation, admin, email, scheduling, teaching, keeping up with the literature, writing papers, building talks, and chasing new ideas. AI has barely reached most of these in medicine, not because it cannot help, but because few people with clinical expertise have set it up to.

This is not about replacing judgment. Judgment stays with you. It is about taking the repetitive, time-draining parts of the work off your plate so you can spend more time on the parts only you can do.

## Why typing questions only gets you so far

When you only type questions into a chat box, three things hold you back:

- **You repeat yourself.** Every chat starts from zero. The AI does not know your specialty, your audience, or how you like things written, so you explain it all again.
- **It cannot see your work.** A chat box cannot open your inbox, your calendar, your PDFs, or last week's draft. So you become the messenger, copying things in and pasting answers out.
- **It forgets.** Close the window and it is gone. Nothing builds up, so you never get a setup that improves the more you use it.

## The four parts that fix this (the Four C's)

You fix all three with four simple pieces. None of them need coding.

| | What it is | In one line |
|---|---|---|
| [[C1 - Context\|Context]] | What the AI knows about you | Plain notes plus a short "about me" file it reads every time |
| [[C2 - Connections\|Connections]] | What the AI can reach | Safe links to your email, calendar, files, and references |
| [[C3 - Capabilities\|Capabilities]] | What the AI can do | Saved recipes that turn weekly tasks into a 2-minute review |
| [[C4 - Cadence\|Cadence]] | When the AI runs | Tasks on a schedule: a morning brief, a weekly digest |

Each part builds on the one before it. Knowing things is no use if the AI cannot reach your work. Reaching your work is no use if it cannot act. And acting still waits on you unless it runs on a schedule.

## A setup that gets better over time

The goal is not one clever answer. It is a setup that builds on itself. The more you put in, and the more you keep it tidy and current, the more useful it gets. The trick is to clean as you go, so your notes stay a clear summary instead of a growing pile. A pile you cannot find anything in is just storage with a search bar.

## What this looks like in your week

Once it is set up, the same assistant can help across your day:

- **Research:** summarize new papers against your exact question, track a literature review, draft an abstract. See [[Research and Literature]].
- **Projects:** keep every paper, grant, and device idea in its own folder with its own brief, so the AI picks up where you left off. See [[Projects and Papers]].
- **Administration:** triage your inbox, draft replies in your voice, turn a messy week into a plan. See [[Administration and Email]].
- **Talks and teaching:** turn a rough outline into slides for grand rounds or a resident lecture. See [[Talks and Teaching]].
- **Ideas:** think through a new device or technique, including early 3D design (CAD) concepts. See [[Ideas Devices and CAD]].

## One rule before you start

> [!warning] No patient information in tools that are not approved for it
> Never paste identifiable patient information into an AI tool your institution has not approved for that use. Use made-up or de-identified examples. The [[Patient Privacy and PHI]] page covers what is safe and what is not.
